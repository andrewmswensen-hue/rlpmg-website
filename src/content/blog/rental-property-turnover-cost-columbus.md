## How Much Does a Turnover Really Cost?

Most property owners think of turnover as a maintenance event, some paint, some cleaning, maybe new carpet. But the real cost is the sum of everything happening at once: the unit is empty, repairs are underway, no rent is coming in, and you’re paying to market the property and screen new applicants.

A [2026 survey of more than 5,000 landlords](https://www.secondnature.com/blog/how-to-reduce-tenant-turnover) by Bay Property Management Group found that roughly 60% reported turnover costs exceeding $2,000 per unit in direct expenses alone. When vacancy loss is factored in, the [National Apartment Association](https://www.naahq.org/) estimates the average non-renewal costs approximately $4,000. For a Columbus property renting at $1,400/month, even three weeks of vacancy represents $1,050 in lost income, before a single contractor shows up.

> **More than two-thirds of rental properties sit vacant for 30 or more days during a turnover. That’s at least one full month of rent that disappears.**

Here is the same math on a single mid-range Columbus rental at $1,600/month, assuming the unit re-rents after exactly one month vacant:

| Line item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Lost rent (one month) | $1,600 |
| Unit preparation (cleaning, repairs, touch-ups) | $1,500 |
| Marketing and advertising | $200 |
| Applicant screening and processing | $100 |
| Total | $3,400 |

That is the good version, where the unit re-rents in a month and nothing unexpected turns up. Let the same unit sit two months or more and the total can double, easily exceeding $6,000.

The real issue isn’t that turnovers are expensive. It’s that most owners don’t budget for them, so every one feels like a surprise. Treating turnover as a predictable operational cost, rather than a crisis, changes how you plan, how you price renewals, and how you maintain the property year-round.

## Where the Money Goes: A Columbus Cost Breakdown

Here’s what a typical turnover looks like for a single-family rental or small multi-family unit in the Columbus metro area. These are real cost ranges based on standard market rates, not worst-case scenarios.

| Category | Typical Range |
|---|---|
| Deep cleaning (whole unit) | $200 to $450 |
| Interior paint (full or partial) | $400 to $1,200 |
| Carpet cleaning or replacement | $150 to $1,500 |
| Minor repairs (fixtures, hardware, caulking, patching) | $100 to $500 |
| Appliance servicing or replacement | $0 to $800 |
| Landscaping / exterior cleanup | $50 to $300 |
| Re-keying locks | $75 to $150 |
| Vacancy carrying costs (utilities, insurance, lawn) | $200 to $400/mo |
| Lost rent (avg. 3 to 5 weeks vacancy) | $1,050 to $1,700+ |
| Estimated Total | $2,225 to $7,050 |

The wide range reflects the reality: a well-maintained property with a responsible tenant might turn for under $2,500. A property that’s been neglected, or where the tenant caused damage beyond the security deposit, can easily exceed $5,000. The variable that matters most isn’t any single line item. It’s the vacancy duration. Every week that unit sits empty adds $300 to $400 to the total, and that number compounds fast.

For context, in-house maintenance labor in the Columbus market runs around $84/hour plus a trip charge and materials. A two-person crew spending a full day on a turn scope burns through $1,000+ in labor alone. That’s not an inflated number, it’s the cost of doing the job right.

### Where to spend, and where to stop

Rent-ready does not mean renovation-ready. The spending that pays for itself is the spending that makes the unit lease faster, and that work sits in a clear order of priority:

1. **Clean.** Non-negotiable, and the cheapest thing on the list. Windows, baseboards, bathroom grout, behind the appliances.
2. **Safe and code-compliant.** Working smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, GFCI outlets near water, secure railings, locking entries.
3. **Current.** Neutral paint, working hardware, blinds that are not broken, lighting that is not dim.
4. **Nice to have.** Accent walls, upgraded finishes, anything a listing photo would call a feature.

Steps one through three drive leasing. Step four rarely does, and skipping steps one through three to pay for step four is how owners end up with a stylish unit that will not rent. Small items in step three carry more weight than their cost suggests: cabinet hardware, LED fixtures, outlet and switch covers, a new bathroom mirror, fresh caulk around the tub. Most of them land under $200 each and they are the difference between listing photos that look maintained and photos that look tired.

Match the finish level to the rent the neighborhood supports. Clean white appliances are fine in a unit renting at $950. At $1,400 applicants may expect upgraded fixtures. Installing $1,400 finishes in a $950 unit does not raise the rent, it just raises the turn cost.

## How Much Should You Set Aside Each Year for Turnover?

Budget 1.5 to 2 times one month’s rent every year for turnover, and more if the property is older or has been deferred. On a $1,200/month Columbus rental, that is $1,800 to $2,400 a year set aside before anyone gives notice. This is separate from a [capital reserve](/blog/going-beyond-6-months-of-rent-in-reserves-how-much-do-you-need-to-save-per-unit/), which covers the furnace and the roof. Turnover money is operating money, and it is spent far more often.

If a rule of thumb is too blunt, the arithmetic is simple:

**Annual turnover budget = (1 ÷ expected years between turns) × average turnover cost**

A unit that turns every two years at $3,000 per turn needs $1,500 a year. A unit with long-term residents that turns every five years at $4,500 needs $900 a year. That version is more useful across a portfolio, where some units turn annually and others hold a resident for half a decade.

### Older properties need a bigger buffer

Age raises turn costs even when upkeep has been good. Original materials often need replacing rather than patching, non-standard trim and layouts make matching parts harder, and older plumbing and wiring produce mid-turn surprises. Code items that passed inspection decades ago may not pass now, and moisture or pest damage tends to reveal itself only once the unit is empty.

| Condition of property | Recommended annual set-aside |
|---|---|
| Recently renovated | 2x monthly rent |
| Minor updates, average wear | 2.5x to 3x monthly rent |
| Poor condition or original systems | 3x to 4x monthly rent |

On that same $1,200/month unit: roughly $2,400/year if it is updated, $3,000 to $3,600 if it has moderate wear, and $3,600 to $4,800 if it still has its original systems. A house built in the 1920s with legacy electrical and original finishes belongs at the top of that range, not the middle. Be honest about which row the property is in. Underestimating the number does not make the turn cheaper, it just moves the payment to the worst possible moment.

Keep the money in a separate account, review the condition of each unit once a year, and ask for a scope estimate before the vacancy rather than after it.

## Normal Wear vs. Tenant Damage, What Ohio Law Says

This is the most disputed topic in landlord-tenant relationships, and Ohio law is straightforward about it, even if the real-world application isn’t.

[ORC § 5321.16(C)](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/chapter-5321) allows landlords to deduct from a security deposit for damages that go beyond normal wear and tear. But the statute doesn’t define “normal wear and tear” in detail. Courts generally interpret it this way: if the damage would exist regardless of who lived there, it’s wear. If it wouldn’t, it’s damage.

Faded paint after a three-year tenancy? Wear. A fist-sized hole in the drywall? Damage. Carpet showing traffic patterns in the hallway? Wear. Pet stains and odor that require carpet replacement? Damage.

> **If you can’t prove what the property looked like before the tenant moved in, you can’t prove what they caused. Documentation is the only thing that holds up.**

The practical lesson here is about documentation, not opinion. Timestamped photos at move-in, quarterly inspection reports, and a detailed move-out walkthrough create a defensible record. Without that documentation, a landlord’s claim of “tenant damage” is just an assertion, and Ohio courts will treat it that way.

Quarterly property inspections (covering smoke detectors, appliances, resident responsibilities, and overall condition) create a running record that protects both parties. When an inspection three months before move-out shows the property in good condition, and the move-out inspection shows new damage, the timeline speaks for itself.

## What a Thorough Move-Out Inspection Covers

A proper move-out inspection isn’t a quick walkthrough with a clipboard. It’s a structured process that determines the scope of the turn, identifies what’s chargeable to the security deposit, and sets the timeline for getting the property back on the market.

It also has to happen fast. RLPM inspects within one to two business days of taking possession, and the inspection produces up to 100 photographs of the unit. That volume is not excessive. It is the evidence behind every security deposit deduction, and it is the difference between a documented claim and an assertion.

### What should be documented at move-out:

**Every room, every surface.** Walls, ceilings, floors, trim, doors, windows, hardware. Photo documentation with timestamps, compared against the move-in condition report.

**All systems and appliances.** HVAC operation, water heater condition, appliance function, plumbing fixtures, electrical outlets and switches. Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, Ohio requires these, and detectors older than 10 years should be replaced during the turn regardless.

**Exterior and grounds.** Siding, gutters, downspouts, walkways, garage, fencing, landscaping condition. Winter damage often reveals itself during spring turnovers, freeze-thaw cracking, gutter separation, foundation seepage.

**Safety and code compliance.** Railing stability, window locks, egress, exterior lighting. A turn is the best opportunity to address deferred maintenance before the next tenant moves in.

The output of this inspection should be a turn scope, a clear, prioritized list of what needs to happen before the property is rent-ready. Structured scope documents prevent missed items, reduce back-and-forth with vendors, and keep the turn timeline on track. An unscoped turn almost always takes longer and costs more, because decisions get made reactively instead of proactively.

## Why Spring Timing Matters in Columbus

If your property is going to be vacant, spring and early summer are the best possible times for it to happen, as long as you move quickly. May through August is the highest-demand leasing window in Columbus. More qualified applicants are actively searching, more leases turn over, and competition among renters is at its peak.

Columbus rental demand has been strong heading into 2026. [Average rents are up approximately 2 to 3% year over year](https://www.rentcafe.com/average-rent-market-trends/us/oh/columbus/), and the supply picture is tightening. [New apartment construction starts in the Columbus metro](https://mmgrea.com/2025-columbus-forecast/) dropped sharply over the past two years, reaching their lowest levels in over a decade. That reduced pipeline means fewer competing units hitting the market, which supports faster lease-ups for well-priced, well-presented properties.

But this window has a flip side. A turnover that drags into June or July because of delayed scope decisions, vendor scheduling issues, or deferred maintenance means you’re burning prime leasing days with an empty unit. Every week of vacancy during peak season is more expensive than any other time of year, because you’re losing not just rent, but access to the strongest applicant pool.

> **A delayed turnover during peak season doesn’t just cost you rent. It costs you the best applicants of the year.**

The practical takeaway: if you know a lease is ending in May or June, start planning the turn now. Have your vendor list confirmed, your scope process ready, and your listing prepared so the property can hit the market the moment it’s rent-ready. ## How to Shorten the Turn Itself

Vacancy duration is the largest variable in the total, so the turn timeline is where most of the money is won or lost. Nearly every long turn is made of delays that were scheduled, not accidents.

**Start at notice, not at move-out.** The clock starts when the resident gives notice, not when the keys come back. That window is enough time to inspect while the unit is still occupied, write the turn scope, get owner approval, and book vendors for the day after possession. A turn that begins with an empty unit and an unwritten scope has already lost a week.

**Pre-fund the account.** Work stops when money does. A scope that exceeds the maintenance reserve waits for an owner contribution, and a three to five day funding delay is three to five days of rent, every time. Funding the account as soon as a vacancy is confirmed removes that delay entirely.

**Sequence the work.** Re-key the locks and clear out any abandoned property first, so the unit is secure and empty. Then run repairs, paint, and cleaning in an order that keeps trades out of each other’s way. Cleaners arriving while painters are still working means one of them comes back, and the second trip is another day.

**Keep a short vendor list.** Owners who self-manage should vet Columbus vendors before they need them, and know their pricing and availability in advance. Calling four contractors during a vacancy to find out who is booked costs days.

**Use a checklist.** A repeatable turn checklist is what makes the process survivable at scale, and it is what stops small items from being discovered at the end. Anyone should be able to read it and know what happens next.

**List before the work is finished.** If the completion date is known and the unit is safe and accessible, marketing can start while the turn is in progress. Photos, a listing, and pre-screened showings scheduled for the day the unit is ready mean applications are in hand when the last vendor leaves, instead of starting from zero.

**Inspect vacant units weekly.** An empty property still needs eyes on it, more often in winter. Weekly visits confirm the work is on schedule and catch the things that turn a ten day turn into a thirty day one: a frozen pipe, weather damage, a vendor who never showed.

## How to Reduce Turnover Costs Before They Start

The cheapest turnover is the one that doesn’t happen. Retention is the single most effective cost-control strategy for rental property owners, and it’s not complicated, it just requires intention.

### Start renewal conversations early

Industry data consistently shows that reaching out 90 to 120 days before lease expiration, not 30, significantly increases retention. At that point, most tenants haven’t started looking for alternatives. They’re open to staying if the terms feel fair. Wait until the last month, and they’ve already mentally moved on.

### Price renewals with the turnover math in mind

If a turnover costs $3,000 to $5,000, then a modest rent increase that keeps a reliable tenant in place almost certainly generates better net income than pushing for top-of-market rent and triggering a move-out. A tenant paying $50 below absolute market rate but staying two more years is worth thousands more than a new tenant at full price after a five-week vacancy.

RLPM-managed leases renew automatically with a 2.5% increase, which tracks inflation and market movement without giving a good resident a reason to start looking. Larger increases are sometimes justified (significant upgrades, a real market shift, or years without any adjustment), but they carry a real risk of non-renewal, and that risk has to be priced against the turn. A resident who declines to renew over a modest increase costs thousands to replace. Residents who go month-to-month after expiration pay a 20% increase, which reflects the vacancy risk the owner is now carrying on 30 days’ notice. For how to choose the actual renewal number, see [pricing a Columbus rental accurately](/blog/rental-pricing-vacancy-columbus/).

A lease renewal fee of $250, which covers the administrative work of extending the lease, is a fraction of what a full turnover costs. It’s one of the clearest ROI calculations in property management.

### Fix the things that actually drive residents out

Some move-outs are life events and no landlord controls them. Most of the rest are preventable, and they cluster in four places: maintenance requests that went unanswered or unresolved, communication that made the resident feel ignored, lease changes or fees that arrived as a surprise, and a property that visibly stopped being cared for. None of those are expensive to fix relative to a turn. Written expectations at move-in, a fast and acknowledged maintenance response, and a renewal offer that arrives early and explains itself cover most of the ground.

Retention is not the goal in every case. A resident who pays chronically late, damages the unit, or generates constant conflict costs more than the turn does. Letting that lease expire, or negotiating an early exit, is the cheaper outcome. The point is to choose it deliberately rather than lose good residents by accident.

### Invest in proactive maintenance

Properties that receive regular preventive maintenance have lower turnover rates and lower turn costs when turnover does happen. Quarterly inspections catch small issues before they become expensive repairs. They also give tenants confidence that they’re living in a professionally managed property, which is itself a retention tool.

The house cleaner analogy applies here: could you handle all of this yourself? Probably. But is it the best use of your time, your energy, and your risk tolerance? For most investors, the answer is no, particularly when the cost of getting it wrong is measured in months of lost rent and thousands in avoidable expenses.

> **The best turnover strategy isn’t a faster turn. It’s a tenant who wants to stay.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does a typical rental turnover take in Columbus?**

Most turns take 2 to 4 weeks depending on the scope of work needed. Properties in good condition with a clear scope can turn faster. For current RLPM averages, check the [live KPI scorecard](/key-performance-indicators/).

**Can I deduct turnover costs from the security deposit?**

Under Ohio law ([ORC § 5321.16](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/chapter-5321)), you can deduct for damage beyond normal wear and tear, but you must provide an itemized list within 30 days of move-out. Deductions without documentation are difficult to defend.

**What’s the difference between a turnover and a renovation?**

A turnover restores the property to rent-ready condition, cleaning, paint, minor repairs, and cosmetic refreshes. A renovation involves structural changes, major upgrades, or reconfiguration. Professional property managers handle turnovers but typically refer renovations to specialized contractors.

**Should I keep utilities on during a vacancy?**

Yes. Utilities should stay active to support turnover work, vendor access, and property protection, particularly in winter to prevent frozen pipes. This is a carrying cost that’s worth budgeting for.

**How much reserve should I have for turnovers?**

Two numbers, and they do different jobs. Set aside 1.5 to 2 times one month’s rent each year specifically for turnover, more if the property is older or has deferred maintenance. Separately, a total reserve of six months’ rent is recommended to cover maintenance, turnovers, and unexpected capital expenses. The annual set-aside is what funds the next turn. The six-month reserve is what keeps a bad year from becoming a crisis.

**How do I shorten a turnover?**

Start at notice rather than at move-out: inspect early, write the scope and get it approved before the keys come back, pre-fund the account so work is not waiting on money, and book vendors in sequence rather than all at once. Listing the unit while the work is finishing, when the completion date is known, removes another week from the vacancy.

**What if my tenant gives less than 30 days’ notice?**

Ohio lease agreements typically require 30 days’ written notice. If a tenant breaks that requirement, the lease terms and Ohio law govern the landlord’s remedies. Consult your lease agreement and, if needed, a qualified Ohio attorney.

**Is it worth hiring a property manager just for turnovers?**

Turnovers are one of the most time-intensive and financially consequential events in property ownership. Professional management brings structured scope processes, vendor relationships, and market expertise that typically result in faster turns and better tenant placement, both of which directly affect your bottom line.
