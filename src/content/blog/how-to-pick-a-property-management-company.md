Choosing a property manager is a decision that affects the value of your asset,
and most owners make it after two phone calls and a gut feeling. This is the
process we would use, laid out as five steps. Peter walks through several of
them on video below.

A note on what follows: this is written to be useful whoever you end up hiring.
If you work the process properly and choose someone else, the process still did
its job.

**The five steps**

1. Determine what you actually need
2. Evaluate the marketplace
3. Research the top ten and narrow down
4. Call five companies
5. Meet with one or two

## Step 1: Determine what you actually need

Before you look at a single company, get clear on what you are buying. An owner
with one condo two miles away and an owner with nine houses in four suburbs are
not shopping for the same service, and a manager who is excellent for one can be
a poor fit for the other.

Work out where you sit on a few questions. How hands-on do you want to be, and
how much repair spending do you want to approve personally? Is the property a
long-term hold or something you might sell in two years? Do you need someone who
can handle an association, a student rental, or a property that needs work
before it will lease? What is your own time worth, and how much of it are you
currently spending?

The answers narrow the field before you have looked at anyone.

## Step 2: Evaluate the marketplace

{{youtube:fnCmIP7sbec}}

The goal here is to build a list of companies with a clear, active local
presence, then cut it to about ten.

Start by finding every professional management company in your market that looks
genuinely active: a maintained website, recent listings, a blog or social
presence that is not three years stale. Discard anyone part-time, and anyone for
whom management is clearly a sideline to sales. Management is an operations
business and it does not run well as a side hustle.

Places to look:

- Who is advertising on Google for management terms in your market
- BiggerPockets
- NARPM.org
- Ask every agent you know for a referral
- Ask anyone you know who owns rental property
- Ask your real estate attorney, CPA or banker

While you are looking, start learning the local vocabulary and the local pricing.
What do leasing and placement fees run? Who keeps the resident's late fees? What
does an eviction cost, and who pays for it? You cannot evaluate a quote until you
know what normal looks like.

Check your state's requirements too. Ohio requires a real estate brokerage
licence to manage property for others, and there is a public licence lookup. Find
out whether the companies on your list are licensed and whether they hold client
funds in a proper trust account.

One rough sizing signal: the number of active listings on a company's own site.
More listings usually means a larger operation. Decide whether that is what you
want, because large and small both have real trade-offs.

Narrow to about ten.

## Step 3: Research the top ten and narrow down

{{youtube:RfS22EPeNV0}}

Now do proper due diligence, and do as much of it as you can before you speak to
anybody's salesperson. You would not buy a property without an inspection. A
manager can affect the value of that property just as much.

For each of the ten:

- Read whatever they have published. Blogs, videos, guides. It tells you how they
 think and how much they actually know.
- Look hard at their rental listings. Are the photos good? Are the descriptions
 detailed? That is exactly the marketing your unit will get.
- Drive past a few properties they manage, if you can identify them.
- Read the reviews, with a clear head. Angry people write reviews, so look for
 patterns rather than reacting to the worst one.
- Search the company name, and search the owner or principal broker by name.
- Verify the licence on your state's lookup.
- Work out what their primary focus is. Single-family? Small multifamily?
 Associations? Commercial? You want someone whose main business is your kind of
 property.

Cut the list to three to five, and write down their phone numbers.

## Step 4: Call five companies

{{youtube:OjAnFmZLEkE}}

Call them all, during business hours, and notice what happens. How long it takes
someone to pick up, and who picks up, is data. You are about to hand this company
your residents' first impression of you.

Go in with questions written down: the general ones you would ask anyone, plus
whatever stood out about that specific company in step 3. If their fees are not
published, that is one of your questions, and how readily they answer it is
another piece of data.

Ask what the management agreement actually commits both sides to, what happens
when a resident stops paying, how maintenance gets approved and billed, and what
their average time to lease is. Ask for numbers, and notice who gives you one.

## Step 5: Meet with one or two

By now you should have a clear top two. Meet them, in person if you can, and use
the meeting for the things a phone call cannot settle.

Walk your actual property with them and see what they notice. Ask what they would
do to it before listing, and what they would price it at, and why. A manager who
tells you a number you do not want to hear, with a reason attached, is worth more
than one who agrees with you.

Read the management agreement before the meeting and bring your questions about
it. Pay particular attention to the term, what it costs to leave, who approves
repairs and at what threshold, and every fee that is not the monthly fee.

Then pick. If two are genuinely close, choose the one whose answers were most
specific.
