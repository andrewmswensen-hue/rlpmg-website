## Why Pet Policies Matter for Rental Property Owners

Many landlords face the same question when preparing a property for rent:

**Should pets be allowed?**

There is no universal answer. Some properties attract tenants who expect pet-friendly housing, while others perform well with stricter restrictions. The decision often depends on the property type, the local rental market, and the owner’s comfort level with risk.

However, one principle applies regardless of whether pets are allowed or prohibited. Rental properties should never operate without a clearly written pet policy.

When expectations are unclear, small issues tend to grow into larger problems. Tenants may assume pets are allowed if the lease language is vague. Landlords may discover animals living in the property months after move-in. Disagreements about damage, cleaning, or noise can also arise when the rules were never clearly defined.

A written policy eliminates much of that uncertainty. It creates a framework that both landlords and tenants understand before the lease is signed. In practice, a strong pet policy is less about controlling tenant behavior and more about protecting the property while setting expectations early.

## Pets Are Now a Major Factor in Rental Housing

Pets have become a normal part of household life for many Americans. According to the American Pet Products Association, approximately **66% of U.S. households owned a pet in 2023**, representing nearly 87 million homes. Dogs and cats make up the majority, but other animals such as birds, reptiles, and small mammals are also common.

This widespread pet ownership has influenced the rental market in meaningful ways. Many tenants now search specifically for housing that allows pets, and online rental platforms often include filters that allow renters to view only pet-friendly listings.

For landlords, this creates a practical decision. Allowing pets may increase the number of potential applicants and reduce vacancy time, particularly in competitive markets. At the same time, animals pose potential risks to property, noise, and liability.

Neither choice is automatically right or wrong. Some landlords prefer to restrict pets entirely, especially in higher-end properties with expensive finishes or shared amenities. Others choose to allow pets because the larger tenant pool can make leasing easier.

Regardless of which direction an owner chooses, a structured policy remains essential. The landlords who experience the most difficulty are often those who allow pets casually without clearly defining the rules.

A well-designed pet policy helps balance two important goals: attracting qualified tenants and protecting the property’s physical condition.

## The Risks of Operating Without a Pet Policy

Some landlords take an informal approach to pets. A lease might include a vague phrase such as “pets allowed with approval,” or the subject may be discussed verbally during showings without being documented in the lease agreement.

While this approach may seem flexible, it often leads to confusion.

Tenants may interpret approval differently than the landlord intended; one tenant may believe one dog is acceptable, while the landlord expected only a small cat. Another tenant might assume that if one pet is allowed, a second animal will be acceptable as well.

When these misunderstandings arise, resolving them can be difficult without written rules to reference.

The most common problems landlords encounter in these situations involve property damage, liability concerns, and unauthorized animals living in the home.

## Property Damage Is a Real Concern

Pets can cause damage that goes well beyond normal wear and tear. Even responsible pet owners may encounter situations where animals scratch surfaces, stain carpets, or damage landscaping.

Flooring is one of the most frequently affected areas by pets. Dogs running across hardwood or vinyl surfaces can create deep scratches over time, particularly if their nails are not regularly trimmed. Doors, baseboards, and trim may also be damaged by chewing or scratching behavior.

Carpet presents another common issue. Pet accidents can create stains and lingering odors that are difficult to remove completely. In some cases, carpet and padding must be replaced entirely before the property can be leased again.

Outdoor areas are also vulnerable. Pets that spend time in yards may dig holes, damage grass, or disturb landscaping.

These problems are not inevitable, but they become much harder to address when the lease does not clearly outline the tenant’s responsibility for pet-related damage. A written policy allows landlords to establish expectations about care, cleaning, and financial responsibility long before a problem occurs.

## Liability Is Another Important Consideration

Beyond physical damage, landlords must also think about liability risks associated with animals.

If a tenant’s pet injures a visitor, neighbor, or delivery worker on the property, legal complications may follow. Dog bite incidents are the most common example, but aggressive behavior in shared areas such as hallways, sidewalks, or yards can also create safety concerns.

Insurance coverage is another factor landlords should consider. Some property insurance policies include restrictions or exclusions related to certain dog breeds or animals with a history of aggressive behavior. If a landlord allows pets without considering insurance requirements, they may inadvertently create coverage gaps.

For this reason, many rental pet policies include rules on supervision, vaccinations, and behavioral expectations. These guidelines help demonstrate that the landlord has taken reasonable steps to manage risk and maintain a safe living environment.

## Unauthorized Pets Are One of the Most Common Problems

Property managers often report that unauthorized pets are among the most frequent lease violations in rental housing.

These situations typically arise when the lease language is unclear or when enforcement is inconsistent. Tenants may bring animals onto the property without approval, assuming the landlord will not notice or overlook the violation.

Once an unauthorized pet is discovered, the situation can become uncomfortable for everyone involved. The landlord may need to enforce lease terms that were never clearly explained, and the tenant may feel surprised or frustrated.

Clear policies prevent many of these conflicts. When the lease explicitly states whether pets are allowed, how they must be approved, and what consequences follow violations, enforcement becomes far more straightforward.

In many cases, the issue is not a dishonest tenant. Instead, the problem began with unclear expectations.

## Pet Policies Also Benefit Responsible Tenants

While pet policies are often viewed as tools to protect landlords, they also benefit tenants who care for their animals responsibly.

In multi-family housing, shared spaces such as hallways, yards, and parking areas must remain safe and comfortable for everyone who lives there. Clear guidelines help ensure that pets are properly supervised and that noise, cleanliness, and safety expectations are maintained.

Responsible pet owners often appreciate these rules because they create a more predictable environment. When policies are applied consistently, tenants are less likely to encounter neighbors who neglect their animals or allow disruptive behavior.

This balance helps maintain a positive living experience throughout the property.

## Understanding the Difference Between Pets and Assistance Animals

One of the most confusing aspects of pet policies involves assistance animals.

Under federal Fair Housing regulations, **service animals are not considered pets**. Emotional support animals, often referred to as ESAs, may also qualify as protected accommodations for individuals with disabilities.

Because of this legal distinction, many typical pet rules do not apply to assistance animals. For example, landlords generally cannot charge pet fees or enforce breed restrictions when an animal qualifies as a legitimate service animal or approved emotional support animal.

However, this does not mean that landlords cannot evaluate requests. Property owners may still follow proper procedures when reviewing documentation and determining whether a request qualifies under fair housing guidelines.

Understanding these distinctions is essential before implementing or enforcing a pet policy. Housing laws evolve, and landlords should review current federal and Ohio guidance to ensure their policies remain compliant.

## Building a Thoughtful Pet Policy

A well-written pet policy should address several practical considerations while remaining clear and easy for tenants to understand.

Most policies outline the approval process for animals, including how many pets are allowed and whether certain species, sizes, or breeds are restricted. Financial terms such as pet deposits or monthly pet rent are also commonly included so tenants understand their responsibilities before moving in.

Behavior expectations are another important component. Policies may specify requirements for noise control, waste cleanup, and animal supervision in shared areas.

Health and safety requirements can also appear in pet policies. Some landlords require proof of vaccinations or compliance with local licensing laws, particularly for dogs.

The goal is not to create an overly complicated document. Instead, the policy should set clear expectations that protect property while providing tenants with straightforward guidelines to follow.

## Pet Rental FAQs

### Should landlords allow pets in rental properties?

Allowing pets can expand the applicant pool and reduce vacancy time, especially in competitive markets like Columbus. The key is having a clear written policy in place before listing the property, not whether pets are allowed, but whether expectations are defined.

### Can landlords charge pet fees in Ohio?

Yes. Pet deposits, monthly pet rent, and one-time pet fees are all common in Ohio rental housing. Ohio does not set a separate statutory cap on pet deposits (as of March 2026), but all financial terms should be clearly documented in the lease agreement.

### What’s the difference between a pet deposit and pet rent?

A pet deposit is a one-time refundable amount held to cover potential pet-related damage beyond normal wear and tear. Pet rent is a recurring monthly fee the tenant pays for the privilege of keeping an animal in the property. Some landlords use one or both depending on the property and risk tolerance.

### Can a landlord prohibit pets entirely?

Yes, with one important exception. Landlords can set a no-pets policy, but they cannot deny a reasonable accommodation request for a qualified service animal or emotional support animal under federal Fair Housing regulations.

### Are emotional support animals considered pets?

No. Emotional support animals (ESAs) may qualify as reasonable accommodations under the Fair Housing Act for individuals with disabilities. Standard pet rules, including breed restrictions, pet deposits, and pet rent, generally do not apply to approved assistance animals. Landlords can request appropriate documentation through proper channels but should review current federal and Ohio guidance before denying any request.

### Do pet policies apply to service animals?

Service animals trained to perform specific tasks for individuals with disabilities are protected under both the Fair Housing Act and the ADA. Pet fees, deposits, and breed or size restrictions cannot be applied to service animals. Landlords may ask whether the animal is required due to a disability and what task it performs, but cannot require special documentation or certification.

### Can landlords restrict certain dog breeds?

Some landlords and insurance providers restrict specific breeds due to liability and coverage concerns. Before enforcing breed restrictions, check with your property insurance carrier, some policies exclude coverage for incidents involving certain breeds, which can create gaps in liability protection if the policy and the lease don’t align.

### What should a rental pet policy include?

A strong pet policy typically covers the approval process, the number and type of animals permitted, size or breed restrictions (if any), financial terms (deposit, pet rent, fees), behavioral expectations for noise and waste, vaccination or licensing requirements, and the tenant’s responsibility for any pet-related damage.

### Should a pet policy be a separate document or part of the lease?

The pet policy should be incorporated into the lease agreement, either as a dedicated section or as a signed addendum attached to the lease. A verbal agreement or informal approval is difficult to enforce if a dispute arises.

### What happens if a tenant has an unauthorized pet?

When the lease clearly states pet rules, enforcement is straightforward. The typical process starts with a written lease violation notice, followed by an opportunity for the tenant to remedy the situation (remove the animal or apply for approval if allowed), and can escalate to lease termination if the violation continues. Clear policies make these conversations much less contentious.

### What kind of property damage can pets cause?

The most common issues include scratched hardwood or vinyl flooring, stained or odor-damaged carpet and padding, chewed baseboards or trim, and yard damage from digging. These costs can add up quickly at turnover, having financial protections like a pet deposit in place before move-in helps offset repair expenses.

### How can a pet policy reduce liability risk?

A written policy that includes requirements for vaccinations, supervision in shared areas, leash rules, and waste cleanup demonstrates that the landlord has taken reasonable steps to manage risk. This documentation can matter if an incident occurs and insurance or legal questions follow.
